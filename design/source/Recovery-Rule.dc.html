Brief

How the Brief reads you

Two signals, compared only with your own recent nights. Weeks set the label. Single nights are noted, never judged.

STEP 1

Your baseline and normal range

Resonance takes the 14 nights before this week and calculates each signal's average and typical night-to-night spread (standard deviation, SD).

HRV range: average ± 0.5 SD, the smallest change commonly treated as meaningful in HRV monitoring. Resting HR: ± 1 SD, so a 1 bpm wobble isn't flagged.

STEP 2

Your week sets the label

The last 7 nights are averaged and compared with the normal range. A signal is strained when HRV sits below its range or resting HR above it.

STEP 3

When the app speaks up

1 night out of rangeNoted. One small suggestion at most.
2 in a rowA direct nudge, naming the likely cause if your logs point to one.
7-night average outThe label becomes Drifting down, with the pattern behind it.
14+ days drifting, resting HR upSaid plainly: a pattern this persistent is worth raising with a clinician. No diagnosis, and no softening.

Nights you tag (alcohol, late meal, hard workout, illness, travel) still count in every average. The tag only changes the wording: a hard ride's dip is shown as expected, not as a problem.

Why "22 of 30", not a streak

A streak resets to zero after one bad night. A rolling count of the last 30 nights loses one-thirtieth. Consistency is what's being measured, not perfection.

What the label leaves out, on purpose

Sleep score. Each device calculates it differently, and it already folds in HRV and heart rate, which would count them twice.

Screen time. It's what Resonance compares recovery against. Putting it into the label would make any correlation circular.

Missing nights. No label until 21 nights are logged (14 for the baseline, 7 for the week). A night without an HRV entry is skipped, never estimated.

A self-monitoring summary, not a medical assessment. Readings from different devices aren't comparable, so the baseline restarts when your HRV source changes.
